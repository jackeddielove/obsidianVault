Submission part 1: Overview
Prompt 1: What is your tutoring experience, and how long have you been a tutor?

My tutoring experience began in 2006 when I was a student at City College of San Francisco. I worked in the on-campus tutoring center, a bustling academic and social environment, where students would work on assignments and take a number when they needed help. There were several of us on duty at any given time. We were always either working with a student or calling out the next number. It was a lot of fun and very rewarding.

After City College, I earned a Bachelors, Masters, and PhD. I was not tutoring during those years, but I taught courses as a graduate student and participated in a variety of mathematics outreach programs. I've always loved sharing mathematics in formal and informal learning environments.

In April, 2025, I started tutoring again to earn extra income. Starting with my neighbor's kids, my clientele quickly grew through word of mouth. Today, I have about 10 students that I see regularly, ranging from a third-grader to a freshman mathematics major at University of Oregon. Most of the tutoring sessions are focused on weekly homework, though some of them are extra-curricular and explore mathematical interests beyond the classroom.

The biggest lesson I have learned over the last year is the importance of relationship. There's a 6th grader I've been working with for several months. Early on, she was completely distracted and disinterested, and I often became frustrated during sessions and would leave feeling I had failed. I *was* failing, but I was also gaining experience and practice. I had to change my approach. Instead of forcing the material and fighting her indifference, I slowed down and talked with her and listened to her. After all, there are plenty of good reasons to "not like math," depending on our experience with it (which is really experience with the people who are delivering it and the environments in which we interact with it). The wall between us came down over the course of several sessions, and I was surprised to discover how well she understood the material. She began to open up and trust me, and suddenly we were working through entire problem sets with no issue, filling in gaps in her understanding when necessary. One evening, we finished the homework early and spent time drawing platonic solids. Her mom told me that she came up to her afterward and said "That was so much fun!"

It really is all about connection. The beauty and the order and the joy of mathematics can arise naturally in the student when they feel safe enough to open up to it.
